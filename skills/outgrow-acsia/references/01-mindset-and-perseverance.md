# Mindset and Perseverance

The behaviors in this system take seconds. The reason they don't happen is never time — it's fear. This file is about the fear.

## Why people don't make the call

A delivery engineer at Continental has a strong relationship with the customer's software architecture lead. They talk weekly about integration defects. That engineer will not, unprompted, say "did you know we also do ISO 21434 cybersecurity work?" — not because they're busy, but because of a set of beliefs they've never examined:

- *I'd be bothering them.*
- *That's not my job — sales does that.*
- *If they needed it, they'd have asked.*
- *If I bring up money, the relationship changes.*

Every one of these is false, and none of them yields to being told so. They yield to **evidence**, which is why the happy customer interviews come first and why their recordings get played to the team. A customer saying "honestly I wish Acsia had told me they did that, we gave it to LTTS" does more in eleven seconds than any amount of training.

## COPE

The four states you are trying to build, in the order they develop:

**Confidence** — reaching out when nothing is wrong. The defining behavior of the whole system. Fearful people wait for the customer to call; confident people call when there's no reason to.

**Optimism** — the structural expectation that the customer will be glad to hear from them. Not a mood. A belief about how the outreach will land, which determines whether it happens.

**Positivity** — pivoting from firefighting to relationship-building. Acsia's delivery culture is defect-driven and escalation-driven; most customer contact is about something going wrong. Outgrow contact is the opposite, and that contrast is itself the differentiator.

**Enthusiasm** — energy on the call. In a market where KPIT, Tata Elxsi, LTTS, Capgemini and GlobalLogic all sound identical on paper, the person who sounds genuinely glad to be talking is not a small edge.

## Helping, not selling

The reframe that makes this work for engineers specifically: **not telling a customer what Acsia can do for them is a disservice to that customer.**

Their software architecture lead is going to solve the ISO 21434 problem somehow. If Acsia — who already knows their codebase, their toolchain, their ASPICE level, their program timelines — stays quiet, they'll solve it with a vendor who knows none of that, and solve it worse. Silence is not neutral. Silence costs the customer something.

For engineers, frame every proactive action as **"helping the customer solve a future technical problem."** Never as outreach, pipeline, or selling. This is not a euphemism — it's a more accurate description of what's actually happening.

## Perseverance: the part built for Acsia's cycle times

Most of Outgrow assumes fast feedback. This part doesn't, which makes it the most transferable chapter for a business with 9–18 month cycles and procurement gates.

**The ten-year sale.** Some accounts take years. The salesperson who stays in contact for six years and wins is not lucky — they are the only one still there. In automotive, program cycles mean an account that says no today is genuinely reachable at the next platform decision, which may be three years out. The competitor who stops following up after two attempts has effectively conceded that window.

**Macro perseverance** is staying with an account across years and program cycles.
**Micro perseverance** is the eleventh follow-up on one proposal, when the tenth got no reply.

Both feel, from the inside, like being ignored. Both are the job.

**Staying in the arena** — the point is not to win every exchange. It's to still be there when the situation changes. Almost nothing is lost forever; most things are lost by absence.

### How to systemize it

Perseverance decays without reinforcement, so build it into the rhythm:

- **Share perseverance stories weekly**, alongside the win stories. When a deal closes, name explicitly how many touches it took and over what period. "This took fourteen months and nine follow-ups" teaches more than the revenue number.
- **Review long-running pursuits in every monthly one-on-one.** Ask which accounts someone is still working that haven't moved. Treat continued effort as the achievement.
- **Consider incentivizing effort directly** — a reward for a sale that took ten or more touches, or that survived five or more rejections. This is deliberately rewarding the input, and it works because it makes persistence socially visible rather than privately demoralizing.

Without this, the natural drift is toward only pursuing accounts that respond quickly — which in automotive means pursuing almost nothing.

## What to do when the mindset work has been skipped

You will be able to tell: participation is low, and the reasons people give are logistical ("no time", "wrong week", "customer's in a program freeze") rather than honest. Logistical objections to a five-minute-a-day activity are almost always fear wearing a costume.

Stop assigning actions. Go back and run happy customer interviews, then play them to the team. Resume when people have heard a customer ask for more. See `09-diagnostics.md`.
