# Roles and Participants

## The five roles

Outgrow assigns five distinct jobs. They are not interchangeable, and the program fails in a predictable way when one is vacant.

### 1. Owner — Jiji, CEO

The single point of failure. Everything else is downstream of whether the CEO visibly cares.

Jiji's actual obligations, which are behavioral and not delegable:

- **Mention Outgrow at least twice a day**, unprompted, in ordinary conversation
- **Attend the Monday huddle**, every week, visibly
- **Write personal commentary on the weekly scorecard** before it goes out
- **Congratulate named people publicly** on specific micro-wins
- **Read the success stories and push them back into the org**

If Jiji does these five things, the program works. If he endorses it and delegates all five, expect participation to sag within the first couple of months — and when it does, the correct diagnosis is not team laziness, it's that the program has no owner.

Acsia should set its own participation floor rather than inherit one; **60% is a reasonable design target** and is used as the diagnostic trigger throughout this skill, but treat it as a threshold Acsia chose, not a prediction about what will happen. See `assets/ceo-brief.md`, which exists to make this conversation concrete rather than awkward.

### 2. Outgrow Leader — the offensive coordinator

Runs the system day to day. Assigns weekly actions, monitors the data, runs monthly one-on-ones and quarterly planning, reports to Jiji.

At Acsia this is naturally the **VP Sales**, with **Akshay operating as implementation lead** during rollout. Worth being clear-eyed: Akshay is an external consultant and cannot hold this role permanently. Plan the handover from week one, and name the internal successor early — a program that depends on a consultant's presence ends when the engagement does.

### 3. Administrator — the analytics manager

Maintains the interaction data, builds and distributes the scorecard, collects submitted success stories and serves them up to leadership.

Unglamorous and absolutely load-bearing. Acsia already has a **CRM Admin** role defined (0.25 FTE), which is the obvious home. If the scorecard doesn't go out on time, the loop breaks — people log for a week, see nothing, and stop.

### 4. Team Managers

Ensure their people understand the actions and actually use them. They are the transmission mechanism between the leader's assignment and the frontline's behavior.

The book's diagnostic is blunt: when managers stop tracking, participation collapses. Managers who treat Outgrow as optional teach their teams that it is.

At Acsia: delivery leads, project managers, and the Pre-sales Lead.

### 5. Frontline customer-facing staff

The people who make the actual contacts. Covered below.

## Picking participants at Acsia

This is where Acsia diverges most sharply from the book, and getting it wrong wastes the whole effort.

**Do not staff this from Sales.** Acsia's sales org is about three customer-facing FTE — one AE, one SDR, one Pre-sales Lead. Running Outgrow through them produces a trickle of actions and changes nothing. They should participate, but they are not the engine.

**The engine is Delivery.** Acsia has engineers, ATG architects, project managers and delivery leads sitting *inside* OEM and Tier-1 programs right now, talking to customer technical staff weekly, holding trust that took years to build and that no salesperson can shortcut. They are the equivalent of the book's field technicians — the people with the relationship and none of the commercial baggage.

Notably, Acsia's own documented sales process has no role definition for delivery staff at all. They appear as "participants" and then vanish at handoff. That gap is the opportunity.

### Roster by role

| Who | Actions they run | Realistic volume |
|---|---|---|
| **Delivery engineers / ATG architects** | One reverse Did You Know or one Did You Know per customer conversation already happening. Text the opportunity to their manager afterwards. | 2–3 per week, zero extra meetings |
| **Project managers / delivery leads** | Reverse Did You Know, internal referral requests, percentage-of-business question at program reviews | 3–5 per week |
| **Account Executive** | Full playbook — proactive calls, all list types, quote follow-up, pivots | 15–20 per week (see note) |
| **Pre-sales Lead** | Did You Know on adjacent domains, technical-problem framing, LiLA attach | 5–10 per week |
| **SDR** | Quote and proposal follow-up on the existing base only. **Not** cold prospecting — that stays in the outbound engine. | 10–15 per week |
| **Marketing** | Testimonial distribution, story collection | Supporting |

**On the five-to-ten-minute rule and the AE.** That budget exists to protect people whose actual job is delivery — an engineer must never feel Outgrow competing with a program deadline. It does not apply to the AE and SDR, for whom proactive customer contact *is* the job; budget them 30–45 minutes a day. If you apply the delivery-staff time budget to a full-time commercial role, the arithmetic breaks: 20 actions logged at 90 seconds each is already 30 minutes before a single call is dialled.

### How to introduce it to engineers

The framing decides the outcome. An engineer told they're now doing sales will comply for two weeks and then quietly stop.

Say instead: *"You already know more about this customer's software problems than anyone at Acsia. All we're asking is that when you're already talking to them, you ask one question about what else they're struggling with — and tell your manager what you heard."*

That is true, it is small, and it is the whole ask.

Give them a physical prompt card (`assets/prompt-card.md`) with two or three rotating questions on it. Rotate monthly so it stays fresh rather than becoming wallpaper.

### Start small

Do not launch org-wide. Pick six to ten people across two or three accounts, with at least one manager who is genuinely enthusiastic. Run four weeks, generate real stories, then expand using those stories as the recruiting material. A pilot that produces named wins recruits far better than a mandate.
