# Diagnostics and Objections

## Reading the symptom correctly

Almost every Outgrow failure looks the same from the outside — actions drop, logging thins out, the scorecard gets quieter. The mistake is treating that as a frontline effort problem. It very rarely is.

Work the diagnosis in this order:

### Participation below 50–60% → leadership, not the team

The first place to look, always. Check honestly:

- Has Jiji mentioned Outgrow in the last two days, unprompted?
- Did he attend the last Monday huddle?
- Did the last scorecard go out with his commentary on it?
- Are managers tracking their own people, or has it become the admin's job?

If any of these is no, that's the cause. Adding pressure to the frontline while leadership is absent makes it worse — it confirms that Outgrow is something done *to* people rather than something the company does.

**Fix:** the conversation with Jiji, using `assets/ceo-brief.md`. Not a new incentive.

### High resistance, or "no time" for a five-minute activity → mindset work was skipped

Logistical objections to something that takes five minutes a day are almost always fear in disguise. Nobody says "I'm afraid the customer will think I'm being pushy" — they say the week was busy.

**Fix:** stop assigning. Go back to `02-happy-customer-interviews.md`. Run them, play the recordings in the huddle, resume after. Pushing through this stage rather than pausing is the most common way the program dies.

### Complaints about admin burden → logging got complicated

Someone added fields. Or the CRM form takes four clicks to reach. Or people are being asked to log inbound work too.

**Fix:** enforce the three fields and the ninety seconds. Cut everything else, even if it's useful. See `08-logging-and-scorecard.md`.

### Actions logged but nothing surfacing → the actions aren't proactive

Check the mix. If it's mostly email, or mostly follow-ups on inbound requests, the program has quietly reverted to what it replaced.

**Fix:** re-teach the proactive line. Audit a week of logs against the eight actions.

### Everything works but leadership is impatient → wrong scoreboard

Someone senior is asking about closed revenue at week eight.

**Fix:** this is a framing problem and it needs answering directly, not deflecting. Show the leading indicators, name the cycle length, and point at the two-quarter horizon agreed at the start. If no such horizon was agreed, that's the real gap — agree it now, retroactively if necessary. See `10-forecast.md`.

### Engineers participating then stopping around week three → framing drift

Somewhere they got told, or inferred, that this is sales. Check the language being used in huddles and assignments.

**Fix:** return to *help*, *ask*, *mention*, *check in*. Never *sell*, *pitch*, *target*, *pipeline*. Have a manager they trust tell a story about a technical problem they helped a customer solve by asking one question.

## Objections you will actually hear at Acsia

Answer these with reasoning, not authority. An engineer who is argued into compliance stops when nobody's watching.

**"I'm technical, this isn't my job."**
> You're not being asked to sell anything. You know more about this customer's software problems than anyone else at Acsia. All we want is that when you're already talking to them, you ask what else they're struggling with — and tell someone. If the answer is nothing, that's a fine answer.

**"I don't want to damage the relationship."**
> That's the right instinct, and it's why we ask rather than pitch. "What else are you working on?" has never damaged a relationship. What does damage it is a customer finding out two years later that we could have helped and didn't say.

**"They'd have asked if they needed it."**
> They can't ask for something they don't know exists. Most of our delivery customers don't know LiLA exists. That's not their failure — it's ours.

**"I don't have time."**
> Five minutes a day, and most of it inside conversations you're already having. If it's genuinely taking longer than that, tell me, because something's wrong with how we've set it up.

**"The customer is in a program freeze / nothing's happening right now."**
> That's the best time. Everyone contacts them when there's a decision to make. Almost nobody contacts them when there isn't, which is exactly why it lands.

**"We tried something like this before and it fizzled."**
> Probably true, and worth taking seriously. Ask what happened. Usually the answer is that nobody senior stayed with it, or the tracking got heavy. Both are things you can point at concretely and show what's different this time.

**"Sales should be doing this."**
> Sales is three people. You're inside the account every week. This isn't about moving work onto you — it's that the conversation you're already in is worth more than any call the AE could make.

**"What if they ask about price?"**
> Perfect outcome. Hand it to the AE. Your job ends at "that's interesting, let me get the right person to you."

## When to pause the program

Rare, but real. Pause rather than push if:

- Jiji has visibly disengaged and won't re-engage. Running without the owner burns the concept — reintroducing it later is much harder than starting fresh.
- A major delivery crisis is consuming the participants. Outgrow assumes a functioning baseline.
- Fewer than four people are participating. Below that there's no peer effect, and the peer effect is the mechanism.

Pausing deliberately, with a stated reason and restart date, costs far less than letting it decay. A program that quietly faded is evidence that it doesn't work here; a program that was paused for a named reason is not.
